Computer vision · Prerequisite reading 02

Find it, count it, outline it.

Two pencils, one ruler, and quite a few ways for a vision system to get the answer wrong.

35–45 minutes, with time to explore the figures and questions · Read after Reading 1

There are two pencils on the desk. To you, that takes a glance. A vision system might draw three boxes, miss one pencil, and confidently call the ruler a pencil too. Even a correct final count can hide mistakes like these.

Suppose we want an app that checks whether a desk is ready for an art activity. Someone takes a photograph, and the app looks for the required stationery. A whole-image label like “stationery” won’t get us very far. We need to decide what counts as an object, where it is, and which mistakes would make the app unhelpful.

The earlier course introduced classification, detection, and segmentation. Reading 1 explored the features a model can use. Here, we’ll follow those features through to the answer someone needs from a crowded desk photograph.

Set aside 35–45 minutes to read, look closely at the figures, and think through the questions along the way. The drawings, scores, and numerical examples are teaching illustrations rather than results from a trained model. Further reading is collected at the end.

What would help the person at the desk?

A student getting ready for the activity probably won’t ask for a complete description of the desk. They want to know whether they’re missing anything. Even that small request has several possible versions.

“Are there scissors?” could be answered with yes or no. “Where did I leave the scissors?” needs a location. And “Do I have two pencils?” needs a count, because knowing that at least one pencil is present won’t settle it. Measuring the part of the image covered by paper would ask for something else again: its pixel outline.

A classification output assigns a category to an image. In the earlier course, the example chose among cat, cup, and rocket. A crowded desk can contain several categories at once. A classifier can also be designed to produce several yes/no labels, one per category. This is called multi-label classification. It still does not say where each object is or how many copies are present.

A detection output gives categories and locations for individual objects, often as rectangular boxes. Segmentation goes down to pixel labels. It’s tempting to ask for the most detailed output available, but that detail costs time to label and introduces more things to get wrong. For a yes/no question about scissors, a perfect outline of both blades may be more than we need.

How much detail would help?

“Is there a ruler?”, “Where is the ruler?”, and “Which pixels belong to it?” are three requests about the same photograph. Which ones could you answer with a whole-image label, and where would you need more detail?

What each request needs

Presence can be handled by a yes/no classifier. Pointing out the ruler calls for localization, such as a detection box. Marking its visible pixels calls for segmentation. These are reasonable starting choices; an existing detector could also answer the presence question, but the request itself does not require boxes.

Three boxes, two pencils

You’ve probably seen an image with coloured rectangles and labels drawn over it. Behind each rectangle, the detector can store a category, a bounding box, and a score. Four coordinates often describe the box: its left, top, right, and bottom edges in the image. Those numbers locate a region in the photograph; they don’t measure the object’s size in centimetres.

Four numbers are a convenient way to point at something. They don’t describe its shape very closely, though. A pencil lying diagonally fits inside a rectangle with quite a lot of desk in it. The box may be enough to help you find the pencil. Counting its pixels as pencil area would also count all that background.

The predictions in Figure 2 go wrong in three ways. The lower pencil gets no box, so it is a miss, or false negative. The ruler gets the wrong category, which can add a false pencil report and leave a real ruler unrecognized. Meanwhile, two boxes claim the upper pencil. Count the boxes and you’ve counted that pencil twice.

The score beside a box can look reassuringly precise. But 0.9 doesn’t automatically mean that 90% of predictions like it are correct. We’d have to compare scores with outcomes across many examples to establish that interpretation. We can still use the scores to rank predictions or discard low-scoring ones, then check which mistakes remain.

Many detection systems remove overlapping, competing predictions after producing the first set of boxes. That can reduce duplicates. It also needs care: two different pencils may lie close together, and we want to keep a prediction for each.

The feature extractor from Reading 1 can supply evidence to a detector. To locate objects, the system also needs to preserve location information and learn to predict boxes. Its output head and training labels must support that task; whole-image category labels alone do not specify where to draw a box.

The count can fool us

There are real pencils and there are boxes labelled “pencil” in the picture above. How do those counts compare? Could a model ever get the total right while finding the wrong things?

Looking behind the total

There are two real pencils and three predictions labelled “pencil.” One pencil is duplicated, the ruler is mislabelled, and the other pencil is missed. More generally, a missed pencil and an extra false detection can cancel in the total. Matching the count does not prove that the system found the right objects.

When two pencils touch

Suppose the rectangles feel too rough for the job. We could colour the pixels assigned to each pencil, as if placing a transparent layer over the photograph. That coloured overlay displays a mask. What the computer stores is a set of pixel labels.

In semantic segmentation, each pixel receives a category such as pencil, paper, or background. All pencil pixels can share the same label. The output says which category occupies each location without necessarily separating one pencil from another.

In instance segmentation, the output separates individual objects as well. One mask belongs to pencil A, another to pencil B, even though both have the category “pencil.” An instance is one particular object: this pencil on the left is a different instance from that pencil beside it.

Put two pencils next to each other so that they touch. Their semantic labels can join into one pencil-coloured region. Counting blobs now gives one, although two pencils are sitting on the desk. Instance segmentation attempts to keep their identities separate.

An eraser lying across a pencil causes the opposite problem. We see two separate pieces of pencil, but they belong to one object. Counting visible regions could count it twice. To label both pieces as one instance, we need enough evidence in the picture and a shared rule for handling the hidden part.

For this reading, masks describe visible pixels. They do not invent the outline behind another object. Some tasks do ask models to infer hidden regions, but that is a different target. The desk project can be defined using only what is visible in a single image.

Segmentation is useful when the boundary matters. Suppose you want the fraction of the picture covered by visible paper. You can count pixels labelled paper and divide by the total image pixels. That is an image-area fraction. It does not automatically measure the paper’s physical area: the photograph’s scale and viewpoint affect how much space it occupies in the image.

A detector may already be adequate for counting separated, visible pencils. Instance masks can add useful detail, but they take more work to draw and do not guarantee better counting. Choose them when the additional detail helps answer the task.

One blob, two pencils

Picture two touching pencils, then one pencil with an eraser across its middle. If you counted connected regions in the category mask, which scene might give too few objects and which might give too many? A quick sketch can help.

How both errors happen

Touching pencils can merge into one region, producing an undercount. A partially hidden pencil can appear as two separated regions, producing an overcount. Instance labels distinguish object identity from visible connectivity, although the model can still predict those labels incorrectly.

The people labelling the pictures disagree

Before a model gets any of this right or wrong, people have to agree on the labels. Give the same photograph to three students and they might return three counts. One includes a pencil because its tip is visible at the edge. Another leaves it out because the tip isn’t enough to identify. The third includes a pencil drawn on the worksheet.

All three may have worked carefully. They were answering slightly different questions. An annotation policy is the written agreement about what to label and how to label it. You’ll often hear reference labels called “ground truth,” but someone still had to decide what to do with that half-visible pencil.

For a manageable first desk project, we could define the task as detecting clearly visible physical pencils and rulers in a top-down photograph. Pictures of objects on a worksheet do not count. Boxes enclose the visible part of each labelled object. A partly visible object is included only when the annotator can identify its category; uncertain cases are flagged for review.

I’d try the policy on a small batch of pictures before labelling the whole collection. Two people can work independently and compare their disagreements. If they keep disagreeing about partial objects, the guide needs examples of which ones qualify. “Can identify” sounds clearer on paper than it sometimes is in practice.

If a photograph contains an unresolved object, you could set the image aside from the first project’s scored dataset and keep a separate challenge collection. Report that restriction. Otherwise, leaving an unlabelled real pencil in a scored image can cause a correct prediction to be counted as a false alarm.

The awkward cases need to be dealt with before the final test. Otherwise, it’s too easy to call a picture “ambiguous” only after the model gets it wrong. A policy can change, but the affected labels and the explanation of the test need to change with it.

Would you count that tip?

The small tip at the edge is the awkward case. Would you count it? Where would its box end? Before opening the example policy, consider what you’d want another person to do with the same picture.

One policy we could agree on
  1. Count physical pencils and rulers that can be identified from the visible image.
  2. Exclude printed pictures and reflections from the object count.
  3. Draw a tight box around the visible portion. Do not guess a hidden endpoint.
  4. Flag uncertain objects for a second annotator. Keep unresolved images out of the scored beginner set, record their number, and retain them as challenge cases.

This policy limits the project to identifiable visible objects. A system tested under this policy has not demonstrated that it can count hidden objects or handle every desk scene.

The train/test separation from your earlier course still applies. Photographs taken in a rapid burst can be almost copies. Splitting them randomly may put nearly identical desk arrangements in both training and test sets. To test performance on a new arrangement, split by arrangement or recording session. The unit you keep separate should match the kind of novelty you want to test.

How close does a box have to be?

We now have some reference labels and a set of predicted boxes. The earlier course could score a classifier by counting correct image labels. Here, a prediction can name the right object and still put the box in the wrong place. Before counting successes, we need a rule for deciding which boxes match.

How well do two boxes overlap?

A common measure is intersection over union, abbreviated IoU. The intersection is the area covered by both boxes. The union is the area covered by either box, counting the overlap only once. IoU is the shared area divided by that combined area.

In Figure 5, each box covers 12 grid squares. They share 8, so their combined area is 12 + 12 − 8 = 16. The IoU is 8 ÷ 16 = 0.5. Identical boxes have IoU 1; boxes with no area in common have IoU 0. A large box around the whole desk can cover the pencil but still have a low IoU with its tight reference box because the combined area is large.

A box can overlap beautifully and still call a pencil a ruler. IoU checks the geometry, so we also need to check the category before accepting a match.

Match each object once

For this example, we'll keep predictions at or above a chosen score cutoff and consider them from highest to lowest score. For each prediction, we look for the unmatched reference object of the same category with the largest IoU. We accept the match if the IoU is at least 0.5. Once a reference object has a match, another prediction can't claim it again.

Under this evaluation rule, an unmatched prediction is a false positive; an unmatched reference object is a false negative. A matched prediction is a true positive. A second box cannot claim an already matched pencil. If it has no other eligible match, it counts as an extra prediction.

There are two thresholds here, doing different jobs. The score cutoff decides which predictions survive. The IoU threshold decides whether a surviving box overlaps a reference box closely enough to match. The 0.5 we chose for IoU belongs to this example; another task or evaluation protocol may use a different value.

Count correct predictions and found objects

Two questions help describe the result. Of the predictions we kept, how many matched real labelled objects? That fraction is precision. Of all the labelled objects on the desk, how many did we find? That fraction is recall. The difference is which group we start counting from.

There are six labelled objects. At the lower score cutoff, we keep eight predictions. Five match and three do not; one object is missed. Precision is 5 out of 8, and recall is 5 out of 6. At the higher cutoff, only four predictions remain: three matches and one false positive. Precision becomes 3 out of 4, while recall falls to 3 out of 6.

In this example, raising the score cutoff removes some incorrect predictions and some correct ones. If we keep the original boxes and their score order fixed, raising that cutoff cannot recover a missed object under our matching rule. Precision may improve or worsen depending on which predictions are removed. A high cutoff is not automatically the best setting.

Two desks, two wrong messages

The activity needs two pencils. On desk A, both are present, but the detector finds one. On desk B, there is only one, but a duplicate box makes the count two. What would the app tell each student? Which message would worry you more?

What the student would hear

In A, the app warns that a pencil is missing even though the desk is ready. In B, it says the pencil requirement is met even though the student needs another pencil. Evaluate these final checklist decisions as well as the detector. Choose the cutoff on validation data before the final test.

I’d also want to see which desks caused trouble. Thin pencils, clutter, and darker photographs can disappear into an overall score. Comparing those groups under the same rules gives us something more useful to investigate. A score from two images is still only evidence from two images, however impressive the percentage looks.

Formal benchmarks can use several overlap thresholds and additional rules. The COCO detection evaluation documentation describes one widely used protocol. The small matching exercise here is not a full COCO evaluation.

A first version we could try

By now, the desk-checking idea has picked up quite a few decisions. Putting them on one page helps: someone else can look at a new photograph and know what counts, what to label, and how it will be scored. A small first version might look like this.

Worked example · A small first version

Check for two pencils and one ruler

Input
One top-down colour photograph of a desk, with identifiable visible objects.
Output
A category and bounding box for each detected pencil or ruler; counts by category; a checklist comparison.
Annotation
Count physical objects, exclude printed depictions, and box visible portions. Review ambiguous cases using the shared guide.
Model starting point
A suitable pretrained detector adapted to these categories if needed. Verify its category set and input requirements first.
Data split
Keep photographs of one desk arrangement in the same split. Use validation arrangements for choices and separate test arrangements for the final report.
Evaluation
Report precision and recall under the agreed matching rule, count errors, and correct/incorrect checklist decisions. Inspect misses, false detections, and duplicates.
Scope limit
The first version does not claim to count hidden objects or work on every camera angle.

The pretrained models from Reading 1 give us a possible starting point, but we still need to check what they return. A detector with “person” and “car” categories may have no category for our ruler. Adapting it could require new training. The amount of data we can collect may be a good reason to begin with a smaller task.

Set a success requirement before opening final test results. Decide which checklist errors are acceptable for this classroom use and under which conditions you will test. If false “ready” messages leave students without equipment, measure them separately from unnecessary “item missing” warnings.

A different desk, or a different project

You could take the same questions to plant leaves, damaged paper cards, or tools on a workbench. Pick whichever interests you. What would go into the system, what would come out, and which awkward example would you want the annotation guide to cover?

For example, damaged cards

Input: one close-up image of a card on a plain surface. Output: boxes around visible damaged corners. Annotation: define damage with example photos, including a rule for normal rounded corners. Evaluation: match damage boxes to labelled corners, count misses and false reports, and test different card designs separately. If the actual request is only to reject any damaged card, a whole-image classifier may be a simpler sufficient output.

The next photograph

A desk that looks ordinary to us can expose several different weaknesses in a vision system. The useful part of the project is being able to tell them apart. A missed pencil, a duplicated box, and a disputed reference label each suggest a different next step.

Here are a few questions worth returning to when you look at the next set of predictions.

A few questions for the next photograph
  1. Why can a semantic mask give the wrong count for two touching pencils?
  2. A detector draws two good boxes around the same pencil. How many can match that reference object in our evaluation?
  3. Why can increasing the score cutoff make a desk checker miss more real objects?
  4. Two annotators disagree about a partly visible ruler. What should happen before that image becomes a scored test example?
  5. A detector gives the correct total number of pencils. What else would you inspect before accepting it?
Some notes on those questions
  1. A category mask does not necessarily separate individual objects; touching pencil pixels may form one region.
  2. One. A second prediction cannot claim the same reference object and is extra under the stated rule.
  3. Some correct detections can have scores below the new cutoff. Filtering them out creates additional misses.
  4. Apply or revise the annotation policy, resolve the disagreement, and flag or set aside unresolved cases consistently. The model’s answer should not decide the reference label.
  5. Check which objects the boxes match, their categories and locations, and whether misses and false positives happened to cancel in the count.

For a first project, I would try the task description on a few photographs before collecting hundreds. Give the same pictures and the same instructions to someone else. The cases where you disagree will tell you which parts of the task still need to be made clearer.

A little further · Removing duplicate boxes

Non-maximum suppression, or NMS, is one common method for reducing duplicate detections. It keeps a high-scoring prediction and suppresses certain overlapping competitors, then continues through the remaining predictions. Close objects can overlap too, so the suppression rule can affect misses as well as duplicates.

A little further · What changes when evaluating masks?

IoU can compare masks as well as boxes. Count pixels included in both masks, then divide by the number included in either. A predicted rectangle can overlap the reference box well while its object mask is poor, so box IoU and mask IoU answer different questions. For instance masks, you still need a policy for matching individual predictions to reference objects.

Measures such as average precision combine information across score thresholds. You can study them after you are comfortable tracing one prediction through category checking, overlap checking, and one-to-one matching.

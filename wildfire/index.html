    WILDFIRE LAB · Lesson 1 of 5

    Where will the fire be tomorrow?

    Define the forecasting task precisely: what goes in, what comes out, and what you are not allowed to know.

    About 60 minutes: 35 on this page, 25 in Colab · No cluster, no GPU

    0 · Course overview

    A map for tomorrow morning

    About 5 minutes

    When a wildfire is burning, the people managing it plan one day at a time: where to send crews tomorrow, which roads to close, which towns to warn. A useful input to that plan is a map of where the fire is likely to be active tomorrow.

    Satellites help. The VIIRS instrument passes over every place on Earth roughly twice a day and flags pixels that are unusually hot: active-fire detections, at about 375 m resolution. The WildfireSpreadTS dataset (WSTS) stacks these detections for hundreds of real US fires, day by day, together with vegetation, weather, terrain and land-cover maps. Our research project builds on it.

    The course follows one question from start to finish:

    • 1 · TaskWhat exactly are we predicting? (today)
    • 2 · EvaluationHow do we score a fire map fairly?
    • 3 · Missing dataWhat if clouds hide the fire?
    • 4 · Fair comparisonThe score went up. Did the method help?
    • 5 · SpecialistsWhen does a specialised model pay off?

    Real satellite data is big and messy, so most interactive parts use a simulated fire world: a 32 × 32 landscape where fire spreads day by day according to fuel, wind and slope. Because we wrote the simulator, we always know the truth, and we can change one thing at a time. Real WSTS figures and the project's real results appear wherever they make a point better.

    1 · Active fire and burned area

    A fire map is not a burn map

    About 5 minutes

    Play the simulated fire below. Green is vegetation (darker means more fuel), the blue line is a river or road with no fuel, and the arrow shows the wind. Switch between what the satellite detects and what has burned so far.

    Try ①One simulated fire, nine days

    Each day, every burning pixel may ignite its neighbours. Spread is faster with more fuel, downwind and uphill. A pixel burns for one or a few days and then burns out. The satellite misses about 7% of burning pixels.

    Pause

    On a late day, pick a pixel inside the burned area that shows no active fire. What can you say about that pixel from the detection map alone?

    Think first, then open

    Only that no active fire was detected there in that pass. It may have burned and gone out, it may be burning but missed, or it may never have burned. The detection map records an observation, not the state of the land. In this course the target is always tomorrow's active-fire detections, not the burned area. Papers that predict burned area are solving a different task, and their scores are not comparable with ours.

    2 · Input channels

    One day, five maps

    About 5 minutes

    The model's input for one day is a stack of maps over the same area. In WSTS these include the active-fire channel, vegetation indices, weather, terrain and land cover:

    Our simulated samples have five channels: fire today (detections), vegetation today (fuel, reduced where it has burned), elevation, and wind as two maps (east and north components). The target is one map: the fire detected tomorrow.

    Try ②One training sample, channel by channel

    A batch of 16 such samples is a tensor of shape [16, 5, 32, 32] = [batch, channels, height, width]. The project's real T1 input has 40 channels per sample: 23 raw WSTS features, with land cover expanded into 17 one-hot channels and a binary fire mask added. The layout is the same.

    3 · Time and information leakage

    What could you have known yesterday?

    About 10 minutes

    The forecast is issued at the end of day t and is about day t + 1. An input is legal only if it would really have been available at that moment. Tomorrow's fire map is used to train and score the model, but it must never be an input.

    4 · Persistence baseline

    About 10 minutes

    The simplest forecast needs no model at all: tomorrow's fire = today's fire. This is called persistence. Any model worth training has to beat it.

    6 · Summary and self-check

    Four questions before you move on

    About 5 minutes

    1. A pixel has no active-fire detection today. Can you label it "never burned"?
      Answer

      No. It means no fire was detected in that observation. The pixel may have burned out, been missed, or been hidden. Say "no detection", not "no fire".

    2. For an input of shape [4, 40, 128, 128], what does 40 mean? Does it mean 40 days of history?
      Answer

      40 feature channels for one day. The number of days is a separate question; a model with five days of history would need a time axis or would stack days into channels, and you have to read the data contract to know which.

    3. Can tomorrow's fire map be used during training?
      Answer

      Yes, as the target: it is compared with the prediction to compute the loss. Never as an input: on a real forecast day it does not exist yet.

    4. Your new model scores below persistence. Is that a failure?
      Answer

      It is useful information: the model has not yet learned anything beyond "fire stays where it is". Always report the persistence baseline next to your model, so readers can see what the model adds.

    1 · Class imbalance

    Almost every pixel is background

    About 5 minutes

    In our simulated test set, and in the real data, fewer than one pixel in a hundred is fire tomorrow. Consider the laziest possible forecaster: it predicts "no fire" everywhere.

    Accuracy is dominated by the background, so it can say "excellent" about a model that finds nothing. For rare targets we need metrics built around the positive class.

    2 · Training a forecaster

    Teach a network to spread fire

    About 10 minutes

    The model is small: three 3 × 3 convolution layers and a 1 × 1 "head" that outputs one score (a logit) per pixel; a sigmoid turns it into a probability. The last two layers are dilated: they sample every 2nd and 3rd pixel. Each output pixel can therefore see a 13 × 13 neighbourhood, enough to notice which way the fire front is moving.

    The loss is binary cross-entropy on every pixel, averaged. Because fire pixels are so rare, we multiply the loss on fire pixels by a positive weight of 5: missing a fire costs five times as much as a false alarm. The official WSTS training code uses the same trick with a larger weight.

    3 · Thresholded metrics

    Pick a threshold, pay a price

    About 10 minutes

    A probability map becomes a yes/no fire map by choosing a threshold. Every pixel then falls into one of four boxes: TP (hit), FP (false alarm), FN (miss) or TN (correct background). From those:

    • precision = TP / (TP + FP): of the pixels we called fire, how many were fire?
    • recall = TP / (TP + FN): of the real fire pixels, how many did we find?
    • F1 = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN) and IoU = TP / (TP + FP + FN) combine both. None of these uses TN.

    Pause

    Raise the threshold slowly. Which number goes up, which goes down? If a fire agency cared most about never being surprised by fire, which threshold would you pick?

    Think first, then open

    A higher threshold predicts fewer pixels: precision usually rises and recall falls. An agency that hates surprises values recall, so a lower threshold, accepting more false alarms. There is no threshold that is "correct" in general. It encodes a cost trade-off, and you choose it on the validation set before you look at the test set.

    4 · Average precision

    Judge the ranking, not one threshold

    About 10 minutes

    Average precision (AP) summarises all thresholds at once. Sort every pixel by the model's score, highest first. Walk down the list; each time you reach a real fire pixel, write down the precision so far. AP is the average of those numbers.

    A tiny example: five pixels sorted by score, with labels fire, no, fire, no, no. The first fire is reached at position 1 (precision 1/1), the second at position 3 (precision 2/3). AP = (1 + 0.667) / 2 = 0.83. A perfect ranking, with all fire pixels first, has AP = 1. A random ranking has AP close to the share of fire pixels, here under 0.01.

    Try ④Precision–recall curve of the trained model (validation set)
    Train the model in §2 first.

    Each point on the curve is one threshold. The dot is persistence's single operating point. AP is roughly the area under the curve.

    AP is the main metric of WSTS and of our project. It has a useful property: it does not depend on the threshold you pick later. It also has a blind spot: two models with the same AP can behave very differently at the threshold someone actually uses.

    Try ⑤Experiment: what does the positive weight do?

    Your prediction: train the same model with positive weight 1 instead of 5. What happens?

    Choose a prediction first.

    The positive weight mostly shifts the probabilities. Without it, the model learns that "fire" is rare and keeps almost every probability below 0.5, so at threshold 0.5 it finds little or nothing. The order of the pixels, which is what AP measures, can stay similar. Report AP and a thresholded metric together, and say how the threshold was chosen.

    Pause

    1. Our run has lower AP but higher F1 than the released weights. How can both be true?
    2. Our AP (0.555) is far above the paper's 0.460. Did we beat the paper?
    Think first, then open

    1: AP judges the ranking over all thresholds; F1 is measured at one threshold. Our model ranks slightly worse but, at the default threshold, trades a little precision for more recall, which F1 rewards. This is the same lesson as Try ⑤.

    2: No. 0.555 is one split; 0.460 is an average over twelve, and the splits differ a lot (± 0.084). The fair comparison for our run is the released Fold 2 weights on the same split, which score slightly higher. Comparing numbers measured on different data says nothing about the method.

    7 · Summary and self-check

    Four questions before you move on

    About 5 minutes

    1. A model predicts no fire anywhere. What are its accuracy, recall and precision?
      Answer

      Accuracy is very high (the share of background pixels). Recall is 0. Precision is 0/0, undefined: say so, rather than reporting it as 0 or 1.

    2. Why does moving the threshold not change AP?
      Answer

      AP is computed from the ranking of the scores over all thresholds. Choosing a display threshold does not change the scores or their order.

    3. You tried ten thresholds on the test set and reported the best F1. What is wrong?
      Answer

      The test set took part in a decision, so its score is no longer an honest estimate for new data. Choose on validation, then score the test set once.

    4. Paper A reports AP 0.46 averaged over 12 splits; your model gets 0.55 on one split. What do you need before claiming an improvement?
      Answer

      The same evaluation: the same splits (or the same single split for both methods), the same target definition and the same AP implementation. Otherwise the difference may come entirely from the data.

    WILDFIRE LAB · Lesson 3 of 5

    No detection, or no observation?

    Clouds and smoke hide fire from the satellite. Test what that does to a forecaster, and whether it can learn to cope.

    About 70 minutes: 40 on this page, 30 in Colab · No cluster, no GPU

    1 · Missing observations

    A zero is not an explanation

    About 5 minutes

    The satellite cannot see fire through thick cloud, and dense smoke from the fire itself can hide it too. In the stored data, an unobserved pixel often looks exactly like an observed pixel with no fire: both are 0.

    A zero in the fire channel can therefore mean three different things: observed, no fire; not observed; or filled in by preprocessing. The number is the same; what it tells you is not.

    Our project studies this problem in a controlled way. Instead of waiting for real clouds, it deliberately hides parts of the input according to fixed rules, while keeping the target unchanged. Because we did the hiding, we know exactly what was hidden, and every model is tested on the same hidden inputs.

    4 · Training with hidden inputs

    Practise with the lights off

    About 10 minutes

    If the model will meet hidden inputs at test time, let it meet them during training. Starting from the model above, we continue training for 200 more steps in two ways, from identical weights:

    • As before: complete inputs only.
    • With hiding: for each training sample, with probability 0.3 hide the whole fire channel; with probability 0.3 hide a random 25% or 50% block; otherwise leave it complete. The target is never hidden.

    The two runs use the same steps, learning rate, data and random seed. The only difference is whether training inputs are hidden. (The project calls these two kinds of hiding FireDrop and BlockDrop.)

    5 · Decision rules

    One decision, five numbers

    About 10 minutes

    Every model now has five numbers: four scenarios and their summary. "Which run is better?" has no answer until you decide what "better" means, and you should decide before you see the results. Otherwise it is too easy to pick the definition that favours the result you hoped for.

    Our project fixed this rule in advance:

    • Primary metric: the mean AP over the three hidden-input scenarios.
    • Guardrail: AP on complete inputs must not drop by more than 0.01.

    7 · Summary and self-check

    Four questions before you move on

    About 5 minutes

    1. A cloud-covered input pixel is stored as 0. Does that tell you there was no fire?
      Answer

      No. It was not observed. Absence of an observation is not evidence that the fire is absent.

    2. We hide part of the input. Should we also hide, or relabel, the same part of the target?
      Answer

      No. The experiment asks how well the model forecasts the real next-day fire when its input is incomplete. The target stays complete and unchanged.

    3. Why test every model on the same hidden blocks (a fixed seed), rather than drawing new random blocks each time?
      Answer

      So that the difference between two models comes from the models, not from one of them happening to get easier blocks.

    4. Method X improves the hidden-input scenarios by 0.10 and lowers complete-input AP by 0.03. Is it better?
      Answer

      It depends on a rule that should have been fixed beforehand: how the scenarios are weighted and how much complete-input loss is acceptable. Report all five numbers either way.

    WILDFIRE LAB · Lesson 4 of 5

    The score went up. Did the method help?

    Separate "more training" from "a new training choice" with a matched control, then read the project's real result.

    About 80 minutes: 45 on this page, 35 in Colab · No cluster, no GPU

    1 · The attribution question

    A better number, an unanswered question

    About 5 minutes

    You take a trained model, change its learning-rate schedule, and give it 3,000 more training steps. Its AP goes from 0.40 to 0.44.

    Try ①Invented numbers, real reasoning

    Can you conclude that the new schedule helped?

    Two things changed at once: the schedule, and the fact that the model got 3,000 more steps of training. Suppose we also run ordinary continuation, with the same starting weights, the same 3,000 steps and the old constant learning rate, and it reaches 0.46. Then the new schedule actually did worse than simply training longer. Without that run, nobody can tell.

    2 · Matched controls

    One starting point, three branches

    About 10 minutes

    B is the matched control: the run that differs from the candidate in exactly one way. In our project's records it is called the ERM control (ERM, empirical risk minimisation, just means ordinary supervised training on the labels). Note that "ordinary" does not mean "without augmentation": B uses the same input hiding as C, because hiding is not what we are testing.

    3 · Learning-rate schedules

    Big steps first, small steps later

    About 5 minutes

    The learning rate sets how far each update moves the weights. A constant schedule keeps it fixed. A cosine schedule starts at the same value and glides down to zero along half a cosine wave: large, exploratory steps early, small careful steps at the end.

    Try ②The two schedules over one continuation

    Why might the cosine schedule help? With a constant rate, the final weights are wherever the last noisy step left them. Shrinking the steps at the end lets the model settle. Whether that helps here is an empirical question, and that is what the experiment below asks.

    One way to write the conclusion

    On WSTS next-day active-fire forecasting with controlled missing inputs, replacing a constant learning rate with cosine decay during a 3,000-step continuation from the same foundation checkpoints raised mean primary AP over three seeds in every history/year cell (T1 +0.003 to +0.008, T5 +0.014 to +0.021; mean +0.012), including the two fixed test years. It is a training-recipe result, not a new model component. Gains varied across seeds, and screening on two other architectures did not pass the project's transfer gate.

    8 · Summary and self-check

    Four questions before you move on

    About 5 minutes

    1. Your method beats the frozen starting checkpoint. What run is missing?
      Answer

      A matched ordinary continuation: same start, same number of steps, same everything except your change. Without it, you cannot separate your method from "more training".

    2. B and C were given the same random seed. Does that make them identical apart from the schedule?
      Answer

      It makes the data order and augmentation the same at the start, which helps. But once the weights differ, the runs diverge. A seed controls randomness, not the starting weights or the training history; those you must match separately.

    3. Why were 2022 and 2023 opened only after 2021 passed?
      Answer

      So that no decision was made using them. If you look at the test years while choosing, they stop being a test.

    4. The cosine gain is positive in all six cells. Can you say it will help any wildfire model?
      Answer

      No. It held for two related architectures under this protocol; the screens on SegFormer and Swin did not pass. Claim what was tested.

    Better than what?

    Specialist models, routing, and the closest control. Then your own experiment, written up so someone else can trust it.

    About 60 minutes on this page and in Colab, plus your final report · No cluster, no GPU

    1 · Specialist models

    One model for every sky, or one per sky?

    About 5 minutes

    So far one model has handled every scenario. An alternative is a set of specialists: continue the same checkpoint separately for one situation, for example only inputs with a 50% block hidden. At test time, a router sends each input to the model trained for its situation.

    This can only work if the router knows the situation. In our controlled experiments it does, because we hid the inputs ourselves. In real use you would need something like a cloud mask, and a wrong routing decision sends the input to the wrong model. Specialists also cost more: more checkpoints to train, store, check and keep in sync.

    2 · The closest control

    Which comparison supports which claim?

    About 10 minutes

    Suppose a "50% specialist" beats the general model on the 50% block scenario. Several different things could explain that:

    • it saw more block-hidden examples than the general model, of any size;
    • it saw only hidden inputs, so it stopped spending effort on complete ones;
    • it was tuned to the 50% size specifically.

    Only the last one is the claim "size-specific specialists help". To isolate it, the comparison must be with the closest control: a model that matches the specialist in every way except the one ingredient you are claiming. Here that is a block specialist trained on blocks of both sizes, mixed.

    ComparisonThe question it answers
    50% specialist − generalDoes specialising on hidden blocks help at all?
    50% specialist − block specialistDoes tuning to the 50% size help, beyond specialising on blocks?
